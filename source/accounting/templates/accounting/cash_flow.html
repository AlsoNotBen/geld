Cash Flow Statement{% notes_pill %}

The cash flow statement shows where the cash came from and where it went in one year. A profit is not the same as cash: a customer can owe money, and a machine can lose value without a payment. Thus the statement starts at the profit and corrects it, until it gets to the change in the bank. It sorts the movements into three groups: the daily work of the business, the purchase and sale of long-term items, and the money between the business, its lenders and its owners. A manager reads this statement to see if the business makes enough cash to pay its bills. Notes to the statement

  1. 1. Operating activities

    This group holds the cash from the daily work of the business. The first line is the profit before tax. The statement then adds the costs that took no cash, for example the depreciation.

    The movement lines correct the profit. A customer who did not pay yet increases the profit, but not the cash. A supplier who is not paid yet has the opposite effect.

  2. 2. Investing activities

    This group holds the cash for the items that the business keeps and uses for many years, for example equipment and vehicles.

    A purchase takes cash out. A sale brings cash in. A large negative total is normal for a business that grows.

  3. 3. Financing activities

    This group holds the cash between the business, its lenders and its owners. A new loan brings cash in. A repayment, a lease payment and a dividend take cash out.

  4. 4. Change of cash

    The three group totals give the change of cash: {{ operating_this|money }} plus {{ investing_this|money }} plus {{ financing_this|money }} is {{ change_this|money }}.

    The change plus the cash at the start gives the cash at the end. This figure must agree with the cash and the bank accounts in the balance sheet on {{ end|date:"j F Y" }}.
